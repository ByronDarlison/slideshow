## Slide 5: Identify cash drivers

### Spoken delivery

The sequence starts with countable activities (cash drivers) that change future cash.

The word countable matters.

A vague ambition cannot serve as a reliable driver.

The activity needs a number, rate, date, quantity, or duration.

The activity also needs a causal connection to cash.

Sales-qualified leads can influence future new business booked.

New business booked measures the value of business won, not cash collected.

On-time delivery influences when completed work becomes ready to bill.

Average accounts receivable (AR) days can influence when customer cash arrives.

These examples sit in different functions.

That is important.

Finance does not own every cause of cash.

Marketing, sales, production, delivery, and collection all shape the result.

The leader closest to the activity often owns the best early warning.

A financial statement line usually arrives after several drivers have already moved.

Revenue is a result.

Sales-qualified leads and new business booked influence that result.

Accounts receivable is a balance.

Invoice accuracy and payment timing help create that balance.

To identify a driver, start with one cash result.

Then ask what countable operating activity creates it.

Ask whether that activity changes the amount of cash.

Ask whether it changes the timing of cash.

Ask whether a leader can see and influence it before cash moves.

If the answers connect, you have a useful cash driver.

### Stage direction

Transition: Map the key functions to find those drivers.

## Slide 7: Quantity and timing

### Spoken delivery

Each driver tells us the quantity and timing.

How much cash will move?

When will it move?

Some drivers change the amount.

New business booked provides a quantity example, distinct from cash collected.

More completed sales can create more future cash.

Fewer completed sales can create less future cash.

Other drivers change timing.

Average AR days provide a simple example.

The sale may remain unchanged.

The invoice may also remain unchanged.

The arrival date can still move by several weeks.

That timing change matters because obligations also have dates.

Payroll has a date.

Rent has a date.

Tax payments have dates.

Supplier commitments have dates.

A profitable sale cannot fund an obligation before its cash arrives.

That is why profit and cash can tell different stories.

The forecast must express both amount and timing.

An amount without a date cannot describe liquidity.

A date without an amount cannot describe capacity.

Leaders should test each driver against both dimensions.

Some drivers affect both dimensions.

The distinction still helps management understand the causal path.

This distinction improves ownership as well.

Sales leaders can explain an amount assumption.

Collection leaders can explain a timing assumption.

Production leaders can explain both volume and delivery timing.

Finance can then translate those owned assumptions into one coherent cash view.

The forecast becomes a shared operating model.

## Slide 14: Write a Situation Report

### Spoken delivery

A red critical number triggers a Situation Report.

Illustrative write-up, not the verified historical cause and correction:

- Situation: Average accounts receivable days reached 42 against our target of 35. Customer payments are arriving later than planned.
- Cause: We follow up only after invoices become overdue. Missing purchase orders and incorrect billing contacts delay payment.
- Correction: The collections owner will check invoice receipt and payment requirements within one week of billing. They will resolve outstanding blockers immediately.
- Follow-up: The collections owner will report progress each Thursday. At month-end, check whether average AR days returned to 35 or below.

Situation states the factual gap. Cause examines the process that produced it.
Correction identifies the change and its owner. Follow-up specifies a date, metric, and check-in.
Keep one report open until its agreed follow-up date, rather than rewriting it every week.
Thursday progress checks continue before the month-end confirmation. Corrective action does not wait until month-end.
At the agreed date, close a corrected situation. A remaining red requires a new report and correction.

The method draws on The Managerial Moment of Truth by Bruce Bodaken and Robert Fritz.
Byron's Scoreboard Day article describes its use in this weekly cadence.
Do not invent the specific historical cause or correction for the 42-day example.
Weekly review does not update the rolling forecast.

### Stage direction

Trace Situation, Cause, Correction, and Follow-up.

Transition: Action corrects performance. Follow-up confirms whether the driver returns to target.

## Slide 16: Review the 36-month outlook monthly

### Spoken delivery

At month-end, update Rolling to see where cash is heading over the next 36 months.

The monthly process begins after actual financial results become available.

Plan preserves what management expected.

Actual records what happened.

Plan versus Actual explains performance to date.

Enter the completed month's financial actuals and review the remaining assumptions.

Rolling recalculates automatically from actuals to date plus the remaining Plan.

Maintain a forward 36-month horizon. Keep the approved Plan visible rather than resetting targets to hide a variance.

Management then asks whether future cash is off track and which future obligations require attention.

The team may change a commitment, adjust timing, protect liquidity, or keep the current course.

This is the monthly process.

Weekly review manages drivers instead.

### Stage direction

Trace Plan, Actual, and Rolling, then point to future obligations.

Transition: The monthly review then shows the resulting cash consequence.
